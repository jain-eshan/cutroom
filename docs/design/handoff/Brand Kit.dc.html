The real problem with a pictorial mark

A scene that sings at 64px turns to mud at 16px. The answer isn't to draw less — it's to design the reduction deliberately: a full version, a mid version, and a tiny version that keeps only the one shape people recognise.

Every mark below is shown at all three, so you can judge the small one — that's the one people actually see.

8aThe setup — recommended
Two people at a table, two booms swinging in.

Your idea, built. The two arms come in from opposite corners and their capsules — the only amber in the mark — hang over each person. It's a recording and a frame: the outer rounded square is both the room and the shot.

Deliberately asymmetric. A first pass had matched booms, matched heads and a centred table bar — three symmetric stacked bands, which the eye reads as eyebrows, eyes and a mouth before it reads as a room. So: one long boom from the corner and one short one from the edge, one person nearer and larger, and a counter that runs flush to the frame instead of floating.

Reduction: scene → booms + bodies + counter → two amber capsules over a line

8bThrough the glass
The control-room window, ON AIR light above it.

The defining architecture of a cutting room is the glass you look through at the people talking — which is exactly where your user sits: outside the conversation, watching it, deciding how it's shown. The amber bar is the only thing that ever changes state.

Strongest silhouette of the six · the ON AIR bar is a natural place to animate

8cThe lamp on the bench
A lamp over the bench, and the strip it lights.

The only mark here that draws what the product does rather than where it happens: the cone falls on a strip, and the lit part is the piece you kept. It's a cutting-room lamp and a selected region on a timeline at the same time.

Most conceptually loaded · the cone needs opacity, so it's the weakest single-colour version

8dTwo chairs
Two chairs turned toward each other, one mic between.

The most human of the six, and the only one that shows an empty room — an invitation rather than a machine. Side elevation keeps it flat and graphic instead of illustrative.

Friendliest · at 16px the chairs collapse toward brackets, which is either a nice echo or a loss

8eThe foam, and a window in it
Acoustic treatment, with a crop cut out of it.

The checkerboard is the wall panelling every booth on earth is lined with — instantly "this is a room where sound is taken seriously." The amber rectangle is the shot you pulled out of it.

Most texture and the best pattern for merch/backgrounds · risks reading as a chessboard out of context

8fTwo booms, no room
Just the two booms, leaning in over a point.

This is 8a with the room deleted — two arms converging on one amber point, which is the moment being captured. It also reads as a pair of quotation marks, so it works as a glyph in running text.

Best at 16px by a distance · least "room", most symbol — the safe pick if you want one mark for everything

Why 8a won, and what the runners-up are still good for
Chosen: 8a — the scene you described, and the reduction ladder holds all the way down to 16px, which is where most pictorial logos fail. It is now applied across the editor chrome, the landing page and every screen.
If you want the strongest graphic rather than the most literal scene, it's 8b. A window with two figures behind it and a light above has the boldest silhouette of anything here, and it puts your user in the right seat — outside the glass, watching, deciding.
If you ever need one mark that does everything — favicon, terminal, embroidery, a stamp on a clip — take 8f. Two booms over a point survives anything.
A combination worth considering: 8a as the app icon and marketing mark, 8f as the favicon and in-app glyph. They're the same drawing at two levels of reduction, so it reads as one identity rather than two logos.
All six are flat geometry — no illustration, no line art — so they redraw cleanly as vectors and survive one-colour printing. For the rebuild in code: the mark is nine rectangles and two circles, nothing more. Exact geometry at all five sizes is in the handoff README.

6 Brand kit — repositioned Not an editor. The whole stretch between a raw recording and a published episode.
What changes with the scope

"Editor" describes a step. You're building the span — the recording goes in one end, a published episode with captions, chapters, show notes and clips comes out the other.

That's a bigger promise and an easier one to explain. It also retires 1a Cutaway: it names one operation inside a pipeline that now has six.

Why the old mark failed

You were right about it. A rounded square with a smaller square inside is arbitrary geometry — the meaning was assigned afterwards, which is exactly what makes a mark feel generated.

A mark earns its meaning by being derived from something real — a notation editors already use, or the literal shape of the product's own data.

What the category does

Search this category and every result is the same four objects: film reels, cameras, play buttons, filmstrips. Plus the current fashion — a gradient blob in a squircle.

All six marks below deliberately avoid all five. None of them depict a camera, and none of them depict film — because you don't shoot film and neither does your user.

The name, under the new scope
6aRecommended
Cutroom

The cutting room is where raw footage becomes a finished piece — all of post, not one operation. Everyone already knows the phrase from "left on the cutting room floor," so it explains itself without a glossary.

Spans the full scope · a real place, so the mark has something to draw · credible to editors, plain to beginners

6bMost distinctive
Postbox

Two words at once: post-production, and the box you post things from. That's the new positioning in one word — everything after the recording, ending in publication.

The pun is the whole idea · warmest of the four · risk: reads as email software until you see it in context

6cMost producer-native
Rundown

A rundown is the ordered plan of a show, segment by segment — which is exactly the object your app builds out of a recording. The product's data model and the name are the same thing.

Beautiful fit for the turn list · "rundown" also means a summary, which muddies it slightly

6dPlainest
Airtime

What you're making, and the moment it goes out. Zero jargon, warm to someone who has never edited anything, and it points at publication rather than at work.

Friendliest of the four · also the most generic — several products already sit near it

Six marks, each derived from something real
Shown at 56px, 28px and 16px — a mark that dies at favicon size isn't a mark.
6eIn & out — recommended
The in-point and out-point brackets, with a person between them.

Every editor in the world already types I and O to mark where a shot starts and ends. That notation is your product: it picks an in, an out, and who's in frame. Borrowed, not invented — which is why it reads as meaning rather than decoration.

Survives to 16px · works as a text glyph in prose · the dot doubles as a record light

6fThe shape of a conversation
The turn-taking pattern itself, straight off your own timeline.

Two speakers alternating, bars of unequal length because people talk for unequal amounts of time. This is the literal data structure the product is built on, drawn at logo scale. Nobody else's mark is "the shape of a conversation."

Most ownable of the six · needs two colours to sing, so mono versions lose half the idea

6gThe room, door ajar
A doorway with light coming through it.

Reads three ways at once: it's the room in Cutroom, it's a frame, and the bright wedge is a crop — the part you kept. Warm and architectural rather than technical, which suits a tool aimed at people who are intimidated by technical tools.

Only works with 6a Cutroom · the wedge simplifies away at 16px

6hThe splice
Two pieces joined on the diagonal — the oldest mark in post.

A splice is where two takes become one continuous thing, and the diagonal is how the join was physically cut so it wouldn't jump. It's the single most accurate metaphor for "raw becomes published," and it predates every tool in the category.

Boldest at small sizes · the diagonal reads as a generic "swoosh" to anyone who doesn't know the reference

6iMany into one
Scattered pieces resolving into one finished bar.

The clearest statement of the new positioning: many rough inputs above, one published thing below, and the published one is the only one in colour. It draws the pipeline rather than any single step in it.

Best fit for the repositioning · also the most abstract — it could belong to a data product

6jThrough the slot
The posting slot — for 6b Postbox.

A letterbox aperture, which also happens to be the exact proportion of a widescreen frame. The one mark here where the name and the shape are the same joke, and the only one that puts publishing at the centre rather than editing.

Simplest to build and animate · closest of the six to an existing convention, so check it carefully
